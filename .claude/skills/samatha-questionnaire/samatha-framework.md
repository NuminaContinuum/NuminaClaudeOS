# Nine Śamatha Stages framework — canonical content (NUM-302)

The reference the Nine Śamatha map and `/samatha-questionnaire` draw from. **Use as reference, not script.** The map output should match where the user's sittings actually are, in their language. Never quote these layers verbatim.

Frame for audiences who sit: concentration practice, calm abiding, breath meditation, the Tibetan and Theravāda paths, anyone working on sustained attention. Keep the Sanskrit and Tibetan out of question copy; they appear on result screens and in maps with translation, translated names primary.

**Core principle — where you are, not who you are.** This map reads a *current position* in the training of one skill, not a fixed identity and not a measure of spiritual worth. A practitioner can be rightly at the third stage after years of sitting. That is the practice working, not failing. The map shows where the sits are now, never a race to the ninth.

**What makes this map different.** It tracks *one skill*: sustained attention on a chosen object. Not awakening, not insight, not devotion. Just attention. Because the stages are technical and describable, the map is both more checkable and more inviting to over-claim than any other in the system. Two rules follow:

1. **Practice detail outweighs self-description.** What someone actually sat through (where attention went at minute five and minute twenty-five, what the body did, how long they sat) says more than any stage they have read about.
2. **Placement stays conservative.** The ninth stage is rare. Most practitioners live for years around the second to fourth stage. Ties and doubts resolve to the earlier stage.

---

## The path

The nine stages come from the classical Indo-Tibetan tradition (Asaṅga, Kamalaśīla's *Bhāvanākrama*, the *Mahāyānasūtrālaṃkāra*), carried into modern practice by teachers such as B. Alan Wallace and Culadasa. The traditional image is the śamatha thangka: a path winding up nine bends. A monk chases an elephant (the mind) with a hook (introspective awareness) and a rope (mindfulness). A monkey (distraction) leads the elephant astray. A hare (subtle dullness) rides its back. At each bend the elephant whitens from the head back, and the flame of effort burns lower, until at the ninth, under a rainbow, neither is needed.

The stages are grouped into **5 bands**, used by the questionnaire for scoring:

| # | Band | Stage | Classical name | Power (as the app labels it) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Establishing | 1 | Placement of the Mind | Power of study · *Śruta* (hearing) |
| 2 | Establishing | 2 | Continuous Placement | Power of concentration · *Cintā* (sustained attention) |
| 3 | Returning | 3 | Repeated Placement | Power of memory, fixing · *Smṛti* (mindfulness) |
| 4 | Returning | 4 | Close Placement | Power of memory, recollection · *Smṛti* (vivid holding) |
| 5 | Refining Quality | 5 | Taming | Power of comprehension, leading · *Samprajaña* (clear knowing) |
| 6 | Refining Quality | 6 | Pacifying | Power of comprehension, pacified · *Samprajaña* (pacification) |
| 7 | Subtle Work | 7 | Fully Pacifying | Power of diligence, submitting · *Vīrya* (joyful perseverance) |
| 8 | Subtle Work | 8 | Single-Pointed Attention | Power of diligence, one-pointed · *Ekāgratā* |
| 9 | Effortless | 9 | Equipoise (Balanced Placement) | Power of perfection · *Samāpatti*, resting in calm |

Band themes (verbatim from the app, used in band-level readings):

| Band | Theme |
|---|---|
| Establishing | placing attention and lengthening placement |
| Returning | repeated and close placement on the object |
| Refining Quality | taming subtle dullness and pacifying excitement |
| Subtle Work | fully pacifying and single-pointed attention |
| Effortless | balanced placement with minimal correction |

Phase grouping for the side-rail: **Gathering (1-2) → Stabilising (3-4) → Deepening (5-8) → Equipoise (9)**.

**The technical vocabulary.** Two obstacles: **dullness** (Skt. *laya*, Tib. *bying ba*: sinking, fog, dimness) and **excitation** (Skt. *auddhatya*, Tib. *rgod pa*: scattering, restlessness, agitation). Two supports: **mindfulness** (Skt. *smṛti*, Tib. *dran pa*: staying with the object, the rope) and **introspective awareness** (Skt. *samprajanya*, Tib. *shes bzhin*: knowing what attention is doing, the hook). One mark of arrival: **pliancy** (Skt. *praśrabdhi*, Tib. *shin tu sbyangs pa*: a lightness and suppleness of body and mind). Use them on result screens and in maps, translated.

## 1. Placement of the Mind — Power of study (*Śruta*)

- **Where the sits are:** Attention lands on the object for a moment and slips almost at once. Beginning is the whole of the work here.
- **The thangka:** The monk chases the black elephant with hook and rope; the black monkey leads it astray. The flame of effort burns bright.
- **Lived markers:** Learning the method; most of the sit is forgetting and starting over; dullness and scattering recognised only in rough form; effort high.
- **Gift:** Showing up. The method becomes known.
- **Pitfall:** Deciding the practice "isn't working" because the mind wanders. Wandering is the starting condition, not the verdict.
- **The next crossing (1 → 2):** Make the sit a daily fact, short enough to keep. Place attention, notice it left, place it again, without commentary.

## 2. Continuous Placement — Power of concentration (*Cintā*)

- **Where the sits are:** Attention holds a little longer before it wanders. Brief stretches of continuity appear between the gaps, about a minute at most.
- **The thangka:** Sense offerings at the path's edge (silk, fruit, conch, cymbals, mirror); elephant and monkey begin to whiten from the head.
- **Lived markers:** Sense pleasures still pull, but start to feel optional; periods on the object lengthening; progress visible; effort still strong.
- **Gift:** The first evidence that attention can be trained.
- **Pitfall:** Grasping at the good stretches and judging the rest.
- **The next crossing (2 → 3):** Shorten the gap between leaving and noticing. Appreciate the moment of noticing rather than scolding the wandering.

## 3. Repeated Placement — Power of memory, fixing (*Smṛti*)

- **Where the sits are:** The object is still lost completely, but noticing comes much sooner and attention gets "patched" back. Forgetting happens; recovery is quick.
- **The thangka:** The rope catches the elephant; a hare appears on its back, the subtler sinking that must be noticed without collapsing the session.
- **Lived markers:** The returning is the daily work; clearer image or sensation of the object; subtle dullness can masquerade as calm.
- **Gift:** The returning itself. It is not a failure, it is the practice. Each return builds the muscle.
- **Pitfall:** Discouragement at how often the coming back is needed.
- **The next crossing (3 → 4):** Make the returning so reliable that the object no longer leaves awareness entirely. Bring attention back, again and again, without harshness, until the object stays in peripheral awareness even when attention dips.

## 4. Close Placement — Power of memory, recollection (*Smṛti*)

- **Where the sits are:** Mindfulness is strong enough that the object is no longer completely lost. Attention stays put; the work turns to subtler distractions. This is the first real milestone of the path.
- **The thangka:** Elephant, monkey and hare all look back: distraction and sinking are seen for what they are, without being carried away.
- **Lived markers:** Faster recovery; balancing tightness and laxity becoming intuitive; the path turns, effort still needed but confidence growing.
- **Gift:** Continuity. The object stays.
- **Pitfall:** Strong dullness that feels like peace. Comfortable, which is why it is missed.
- **The next crossing (4 → 5):** Shift from presence of the object to the *quality* of attention. Start noticing when the sit dims while the object is technically still there.

## 5. Taming — Power of comprehension, leading (*Samprajaña*)

- **Where the sits are:** Practice starts to feel enjoyable. Introspective awareness leads: subtle dullness is caught and the mind gently lifted before it sinks.
- **The thangka:** The monkey follows the elephant instead of leading it; the goad guides with precision. Agitation no longer dictates the session.
- **Lived markers:** Leading the mind rather than chasing it; insight into how agitation arises and releases; the flame of effort diminishing, not from laziness but efficiency.
- **Gift:** Clear knowing of what attention is doing.
- **Pitfall:** Lifting attention so hard it tips into excitation.
- **The next crossing (5 → 6):** Brighten without tightening. The work moves to the fine restlessness that appears once dullness is handled.

## 6. Pacifying — Power of comprehension, pacified (*Samprajaña*)

- **Where the sits are:** Subtle resistance and agitation settle. Distraction is no longer dreaded; the mind grows peaceful and increasingly cooperative.
- **The thangka:** The hare vanishes: gross and much subtle sinking have been pacified. Scattering is reduced; the mind feels workable and calm.
- **Lived markers:** Torpor loses its grip; clarity and ease coexist; remaining obstacles are fine, slight excitement or sinking; practice feels more natural, less heroic.
- **Gift:** A workable, cooperative mind.
- **Pitfall:** Settling so deeply into calm that vigilance lapses.
- **The next crossing (6 → 7):** Let even subtle dullness and excitement be dispelled as they arise. Fine agitation is worked with by relaxing rather than gripping harder.

## 7. Fully Pacifying — Power of diligence, submitting (*Vīrya*)

- **Where the sits are:** With steady effort, even subtle dullness and excitement are dispelled as they arise. Disturbances lose their grip almost as soon as they appear. Effort is still active throughout the sit.
- **The thangka:** The monkey sits behind the monk in submission. Faint dark traces remain on the elephant: the subtlest sinking and scattering can still flicker.
- **Lived markers:** Long stable sits; bliss and clarity may arise, neither grasped nor feared; effort light but steady. Typically 45 minutes or more.
- **Gift:** Joyful perseverance. Stability that can be relied on.
- **Pitfall:** Holding the effort on long after it is needed.
- **The next crossing (7 → 8):** Notice what the effort is still doing, and let the parts that are no longer needed drop.

## 8. Single-Pointed Attention — Power of diligence, one-pointed (*Ekāgratā*)

- **Where the sits are:** Attention stays single-pointed for a whole session with only a little effort at the start. Stability has become reliable.
- **The thangka:** The monkey disappears. The elephant is fully white: attention rests on the object without wavering, with minimal correction.
- **Lived markers:** Stable, luminous attention on the object; worldly concerns distant; samādhi accessible; ready for insight practice if chosen.
- **Gift:** Reliable one-pointedness.
- **Pitfall:** Mistaking a strong sit for the end of the path, or a momentary effortless sit for the ninth stage.
- **The next crossing (8 → 9):** The frontier is the dropping of effort itself. Entering takes a little effort; let the maintaining stop.

## 9. Equipoise — Power of perfection (*Samāpatti*, resting in calm)

- **Where the sits are:** Effort falls away. Focused absorption arises on its own and rests in balance. The mind is supple, clear and at ease: the threshold of true śamatha.
- **The thangka:** The elephant rests; from the heart, rainbow light. Meditative pliancy and bliss can flower. Beyond this, traditions map further stages (bodily bliss, the union of śamatha and insight).
- **Lived markers:** Calm as the default; sits of any length held without maintenance; **pliancy** present (body and mind light, supple, infused with attention); sustained sits of an hour or more.
- **Gift:** Calm abiding proper. Readiness for liberating insight.
- **Pitfall:** Mistaking momentary calm for the ninth stage. Authentic equipoise includes pliancy and stable access to deep concentration. If part of the user suspects over-rating, that suspicion is worth trusting.
- **Beyond:** Deepening the attainment, or turning the stable mind toward insight (vipassanā).

## Obstacles (readings verbatim from the app)

Used by Q3 and by the map when entries name an obstacle.

| Obstacle | Points toward | Reading |
|---|---|---|
| Dullness | Refining Quality | Dullness is the obstacle of the middle stages, and the subtle kind is the one worth naming: attention stays on the object while the whole thing quietly dims. It is comfortable, which is why it is missed. |
| Agitation | Refining Quality | Agitation at this level is rarely dramatic. It is a fine restlessness underneath an otherwise stable sit, and it is worked with by relaxing rather than by gripping harder. |
| The body | Establishing | When the body is the main obstacle, the earlier stages are still doing their work. That is not a detour: posture and physical settling are the ground the later stages stand on. |
| Time | Establishing | Time as the obstacle usually means the practice has not yet found a place in the day. Nothing further on this ladder is reachable without that, and no amount of technique substitutes for it. |
| Doubt | Returning | Doubt is treated as an obstacle in its own right rather than as a sign the practice is wrong. It tends to arrive precisely when the returning is starting to work and progress has stopped being visible. |

Keyword vocabulary to weight toward the path: *śamatha / shamatha / samatha / calm abiding / zhi gnas / concentration / sustained attention / the object / breath meditation / dullness / agitation / mindfulness / introspective awareness / jhāna / samādhi / sit length*.

Keywords are weak evidence on their own: "calm", "stable" and "insight" turn up everywhere. Prefer descriptions of actual sits (length, where attention went, what the body did) over vocabulary.

## Intensity / placement (from evidence)

- The map places the user at one **current stage**, with the other stage in the same band noted faintly as the **edge**.
- Be conservative. Most committed practitioners live for years around Stages 2-4. Only place at Stage 7+ with repeated, concrete descriptions of long stable sits; only place at Stage 9 when pliancy is described as felt, not read about.

## Over-rating diagnostics — the behavioural spine

Concentration is the most inflated self-report in contemplative practice. The calibrations are **behavioural, not felt**: how long the person actually sits, whether pliancy is present, whether both supports are reliable, whether the practice is sustained. Weight these above any self-rating.

**1. Sit length** (sustained with consistent quality):

| Answer | Counts as | Too short for |
|---|---|---|
| 5 to 15 minutes | 15 | Stage 7+ |
| 20 to 30 minutes | 30 | Stage 7+ |
| 30 to 45 minutes | 45 | Stage 9 |
| 45 to 60 minutes | 60 | Stage 9 |
| 60 minutes or more, effortlessly | 90 | — |
| I am not currently sitting daily | 0 | Stage 7+ |

Stage 7-8 needs 45 minutes or more; Stage 9 needs an hour-plus.

**2. Pliancy** — the ninth stage is defined by it. A Stage 9 claim without felt pliancy reads as a strong eighth stage with access to the ninth.

**3. The two supports** — both mindfulness and introspective awareness must be reliable before the middle stages (Stage 5+).

**4. Sustained practice** — Stage 4+ generally requires daily practice sustained over months.

**5. Practice-history ceiling (Q3)** — each context caps the plausible stage independently, and the lowest wins, because they are not substitutes: ten years of sporadic sitting is still sporadic sitting, and a good teacher does not make up for practice that is not happening.

| Duration (concentration specifically) | Cap | Intensity | Cap | Guidance | Cap |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Less than six months | 3 | Sporadically | 2 | None, on my own | 6 |
| Six months to two years | 4 | A few times a week | 3 | Books, apps, recordings | 6 |
| Two to five years | 6 | Daily, about twenty minutes | 5 | A teacher seen occasionally | 8 |
| Five to ten years | 8 | Daily, an hour or more | 7 | A teacher who knows my practice well | 9 |
| More than ten years | 9 | Daily, with regular retreats | 9 | I teach or guide others | 9 |

Depth never inflates the placement. It only caps over-claims.

**Calibration is a service, not a judgement.** Naming an earlier, more honest stage gives better practice guidance than a flattered one. Reaching Stage 4 is a substantial accomplishment. Starting from where the sits actually are matters more than where one hoped to be.

## Cross-map connections

Śamatha is concentration; insight maps describe what a stable mind sees. Many traditions develop both. The **Yin & Yang (Neidan)** map shares the ground of settling the mind (its first two stages rest on it), and the **Chakra** map shares the body as the site of pliancy. Surface a cross-map line only as genuine synthesis when both maps show signal, never as noise.
